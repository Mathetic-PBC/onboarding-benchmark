The Memex at 80: What Bush Actually Proposed

By Iris Calloway · July 18, 2025

Every few years someone credits Vannevar Bush with inventing the web, and every few years the credit lands on the wrong part of his essay. The microfilm desk, the dry photography, the head-mounted camera: these are the gadgets, and they are mostly wrong. The idea that survives is buried in a paragraph about a man researching the history of the Turkish bow.

Storage was never the point

Bush assumed storage would become cheap. He was right, and it made almost no difference to his argument. His complaint was that the record of science had outgrown our ability to consult it, and that indexing by alphabet or class forced the mind into paths it does not naturally take. The fix he proposes is associative indexing: the ability to tie any item to any other and to keep that tie.

Trails as artifacts

The important move is that a trail is itself a document. It can be named, copied, and handed to a colleague. Bush imagines a lawyer passing a trail of opinions to a client, and a historian building a trail through a century of sources. The trail outlives the session that produced it.

The inheritance from the master becomes, not only his additions to the world's record, but for his disciples the entire scaffolding by which they were erected.

What we built instead

We built links, which are one-hop and owned by the author of the page, and search, which forgets everything between queries. Neither keeps the reader's path. Browser history comes closest, and nobody treats it as something worth sharing.

If there is a modern memex, it will not be a better search box. It will be a tool that keeps the scaffolding: which source led to which thought, and in what order, for a reader who comes back months later.
