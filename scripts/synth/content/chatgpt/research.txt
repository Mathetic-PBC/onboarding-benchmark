@assistant
Two separate problems here:

**1. Why it disconnects (code 1006, ~40 s):** 1006 is an abnormal close with no close frame, which usually means something in the middle killed an idle connection. ~40 s of silence lines up with a proxy/load-balancer idle timeout. If section B is on a different network segment or goes through a different proxy (e.g., the lab's wifi vs wired, or a campus proxy), that explains A vs B.

Quick fix: send an application-level heartbeat more often than the timeout.

```js
// client
let hb;
socket.addEventListener("open", () => {
  hb = setInterval(() => socket.readyState === 1 && socket.send(JSON.stringify({ t: "ping" })), 20000);
});
socket.addEventListener("close", () => clearInterval(hb));
```

If you use socket.io, set `pingInterval: 15000, pingTimeout: 10000` on the server instead.

**2. Why you lose events (the bigger bug):** your log says `flush: 0 queued events` after reconnect, so events emitted while the socket was closed aren't being queued. Probably your `send()` does `if (socket.readyState === OPEN) socket.send(...)` and silently drops otherwise.

```js
const queue = [];
function emit(evt) {
  if (socket && socket.readyState === WebSocket.OPEN) socket.send(JSON.stringify(evt));
  else queue.push(evt);
}
function flush() {
  while (queue.length && socket.readyState === WebSocket.OPEN) socket.send(JSON.stringify(queue.shift()));
}
socket.addEventListener("open", flush);
```

Ship #2 first. It stops the data loss even if the disconnects continue.

### CONV ise6664-appraisal
title: Appraisal theory explained simply
start: 2026-02-03 21:20
projects: project_ise6664_affective_computing
model: gpt-5-3
notes: ISE 6664 (Dr. Alan Brooks) reading response help
---
@user
for ise 6664 (affective computing) we have to write a 1 page reading response on appraisal theory (scherer, OCC model). explain it like im smart but havent read it. how does it relate to detecting frustration in tutoring
@assistant
**Appraisal theory in one paragraph:** emotions aren't triggered directly by events but by how a person *evaluates* an event relative to their goals. The same event (a failing test) produces frustration in one student and curiosity in another, depending on appraisals like: Is this relevant to my goals? Is it consistent with what I wanted? Who/what caused it? Can I cope with it? Is it fair?

**Scherer's Component Process Model** formalizes this as a sequence of *stimulus evaluation checks*: novelty → intrinsic pleasantness → goal relevance/conduciveness → coping potential → norm compatibility. The emotion is the pattern of outcomes across those checks.

**OCC (Ortony, Clore & Collins)** is a structural taxonomy: 22 emotion types organized by whether you're reacting to *events* (desirable/undesirable for goals), *agents' actions* (praiseworthy/blameworthy), or *objects* (appealing/unappealing). It became popular in computing because it's rule-like and implementable.

**Connection to frustration in tutoring:**
- Frustration ≈ goal-relevant + goal-obstructive + uncertain/low coping potential. In a CS tutoring transcript, that maps to: student is trying to complete the task (relevance), something blocks it (error, unhelpful hint), and they don't see how to proceed (coping).
- That suggests features beyond sentiment words: repeated failed attempts (obstruction), hint requests after hints (low coping), language attributing blame to the system ("this tool is useless") versus self.
- Appraisal theory also predicts *why frustration isn't always bad*. High coping potential turns obstruction into challenge, which is the "productive struggle" idea.

A good reading-response angle: sentiment classifiers detect valence, but appraisal theory says frustration is about *goal obstruction × coping*, so a detector should model task state, not just words.
@user
thats really good. is OCC actually used in modern affect detection or is it outdated
@assistant
Mostly as a design vocabulary now, less as a computational model:

- **Agent/game AI and virtual humans:** OCC-style appraisal still drives emotion generation for characters.
- **Affect *detection* (sensing a person's state):** dominated by data-driven classifiers on facial, physiological, interaction-log or text features, often with dimensional (valence/arousal) or education-specific labels (boredom, confusion, frustration, engaged concentration). Those education labels come from the affect-in-learning literature more than from OCC.
- **Where appraisal comes back:** in feature design and interpretation. It explains *which* log features should matter (obstruction, coping) and helps interpret errors.

So for your response: OCC is "outdated as an algorithm, useful as a theory of what to measure."

we incorporated mathetic as a delaware public benefit corporation. what do we actually have to do differently vs a normal c corp. be concise, dont speculate on legal stuff you're not sure about
@assistant
At a high level (confirm specifics with your lawyer/registered agent):

1. **Stated public benefit.** Your certificate of incorporation names a specific public benefit. Directors must balance stockholders' financial interests, the interests of those materially affected, and that benefit.
2. **Biennial benefit report to stockholders.** Delaware requires a PBC to give stockholders a statement on its promotion of the public benefit at least every two years. It doesn't have to be public or third-party certified unless your charter says so.
3. **Name / notices.** PBC status must be disclosed (e.g., "PBC" in the name, which you have, and in stock certificates/notices).
4. **Same as any Delaware corp:** annual franchise tax + annual report to the state, due **March 1** each year. Use the assumed par value capital method for franchise tax; the default authorized-shares method can produce a scary-looking bill for startups.
5. **Investors:** some investors ask about the PBC structure. Most early-stage investors are fine with it, but be ready to explain your benefit statement in one sentence.

If you incorporated in 2026, yes. The first annual report and franchise tax payment are due by **March 1, 2027**, covering calendar year 2026. Put it on the calendar with a reminder in mid-February. Your registered agent will likely email you too.

### CONV augcog-licklider-engelbart
title: Licklider vs Engelbart vs Bush
start: 2026-02-08 22:40

@user
reading group this week is bush (as we may think), licklider (man-computer symbiosis), engelbart (augmenting human intellect). give me the one-sentence core of each + the main tension between them. i have to lead discussion
@assistant
**Bush (1945), "As We May Think":** the problem is that the record of knowledge has outgrown our ability to consult it, so build a personal device (the memex) that stores your materials and lets you link them into *associative trails* that you and others can follow later.

**Licklider (1960), "Man-Computer Symbiosis":** humans and computers should be coupled tightly in real time, with the computer doing the routinizable work so that people can do formulating, goal-setting and judgment, as a partnership rather than humans just programming machines.

**Engelbart (1962), "Augmenting Human Intellect":** human capability comes from a *system* of people, language, artifacts, methods and training (H-LAM/T), and we should deliberately redesign that whole system, with the computer as a central artifact, to raise our ability to deal with complex problems.

**Main tensions for discussion:**
1. **Storage vs. partnership vs. system.** Bush is about *retrieval and trails*, Licklider about *division of labor in real time*, Engelbart about *co-evolving the human + tool system* (including training and methods).
2. **Who adapts?** Licklider imagines the machine fitting human thought. Engelbart expects humans to learn new methods (the "bootstrapping" idea, and the willingness to accept a learning curve).
3. **Individual vs. collective.** Bush's memex is personal (trails shared later). Engelbart's lab was about *collaborative* knowledge work.

A good opening question: "Today's AI chat assistants: are they a memex, a symbiotic partner, or an augmentation system? What's missing from each frame?"
@user
whats missing — my take is trails. chats dont keep trails. is that a fair reading of bush
@assistant
Yes, it's a fair and pointed reading. Bush's emphasis wasn't just on storage but on the **trail as a durable, shareable artifact**: you could name a trail, return to it, and hand it to a colleague. Chat assistants produce a transcript, but the reasoning path *across* sources (which paper led to which question led to which idea) isn't captured or linkable. A transcript is closer to a diary than a trail.

A useful nuance for discussion: Bush's trails were *authored* by the user deliberately. Any system that infers trails automatically is doing something Bush didn't describe, and that raises its own questions about accuracy and ownership.
