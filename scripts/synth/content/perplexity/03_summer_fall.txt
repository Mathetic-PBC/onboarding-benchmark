### CONV student-level-leakage-edm
title: Student-level data leakage in educational ML
start: 2026-05-04 23:30
projects: project_idetrace
---
@user
for a dataset paper on student coding sessions (30s windows), should train/test be split by window, session, or student? what do EDM papers do
@assistant
**Split by student** for the main benchmark split [1][2]. Windows from the same student share idiosyncrasies (typing rhythm, coding style, which bugs they make), so a window-level or even session-level random split lets a model recognize the *student* rather than the behavior, inflating scores.

What the literature shows [1][2][3]:
- Studies that compared splits on educational log data report sizeable drops in performance when moving from random-row to student-level splits; the gap is larger for models with many features or deep models that can memorize individuals [1].
- Many EDM/L@S papers now use **grouped cross-validation by student** (e.g., `GroupKFold` with student id as the group) and call out leakage explicitly [2].
- Ostrowski et al. (L@S '25) reported both a session-level result and the drop when same-student windows were kept out of the comparison baseline, precisely to make leakage visible [3].

For a NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks submission, reviewers will also look for: the split files released with the dataset, the grouping variable documented in the datasheet, and baselines reported on the official split [2].
[src] The cost of ignoring student identity in educational data splits | https://www.edmreviewjournal.org/student-level-splits | Performance comparison of random-row vs student-grouped splits on log data.
[src] Evaluation practices in educational data mining: a review | https://www.edmreviewjournal.org/evaluation-practices | Grouped cross-validation and leakage reporting in recent EDM papers.
[src] Productive Struggle or Silent Stalling? (Ostrowski et al., L@S '25) | https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3698205.3729841 | Reports the drop when same-student windows are excluded from the baseline.
@user +4m
in sklearn whats the cleanest way. i also want the label distribution roughly balanced across folds
@assistant
Use **`StratifiedGroupKFold`** (scikit-learn ≥ 1.0) [1]:

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedGroupKFold
cv = StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
for train_idx, test_idx in cv.split(X, y, groups=df["student_id"]):
    assert set(df.student_id.iloc[train_idx]).isdisjoint(df.student_id.iloc[test_idx])
```

It keeps each student in exactly one fold while trying to balance class proportions. For a single fixed train/test split, take one fold as test, or use `GroupShuffleSplit` and check the label distribution afterwards [1][2]. Keep the assertion in your pipeline — it's the cheapest leakage test there is.
[src] scikit-learn — StratifiedGroupKFold | https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.StratifiedGroupKFold.html | Cross-validator with non-overlapping groups and stratification.
[src] Practical leakage checks for ML pipelines | https://www.mlpitfalls.net/leakage-checks | Assertions and audits to catch train/test contamination.
@user +2m
should i also group by course section or is student enough
@assistant
Student-level grouping is the minimum. If you want to claim generalization to **new classrooms**, also report a **leave-one-section-out** (or leave-one-course-out) split as a secondary evaluation, since sections differ in instructor, assignment timing and TA behavior [1]. Many benchmark papers report an "in-distribution" student split and an "out-of-distribution" section/course split side by side [1][2].
[src] Evaluation practices in educational data mining: a review | https://www.edmreviewjournal.org/evaluation-practices | Out-of-distribution evaluation by course or section.
[src] Datasets and benchmarks for learning analytics: recommended splits | https://www.learninganalyticsbench.org/recommended-splits | ID vs OOD splits in education benchmarks.

### CONV rebuttal-generalization-one-course
title: Responding to "only one course" in a rebuttal
start: 2026-06-16 00:20
projects: project_tutortrace
---
@user
uist reviewers say our study is one course so generalization is unclear. rebuttal is 2 days. what's a credible response that isnt overpromising
@assistant
A credible rebuttal on generalization usually combines **acknowledgment, evidence you already have, and a bounded commitment** [1][2]:

1. **Acknowledge directly**: "We agree a single-course deployment limits claims about generalization."
2. **Point to existing evidence**, if any: multiple sections, different TAs, variation in assignments, or a second smaller dataset. Even a modest additional analysis (e.g., results split by section) shows robustness within the data you have.
3. **Commit to something feasible for camera-ready**: e.g., "We will add a cross-course analysis on [dataset] and an explicit Limitations section scoping our claims to CS1-style courses." Only promise analyses you can finish in the camera-ready window [2].
4. **Reframe the contribution** if appropriate: for a systems paper, the claim may be that the *technique* works, with deployment as a demonstration rather than a population-level finding [1].

Avoid promising new user studies you can't run in time; program committees notice and it can hurt trust in the rest of the rebuttal [2].
[src] Writing effective HCI rebuttals | https://www.hcipapersguide.org/rebuttals | Structure and tone for responding to reviewer concerns.
[src] What to promise (and not) in a conditional-accept rebuttal | https://www.academicwritingguide.org/rebuttal-promises | Feasible commitments for camera-ready revisions.

reviewers also asked about label reliability. we have fleiss 0.71, 3 raters. enough to just state it?
@assistant
State it with context: "Three raters labeled all windows; agreement was substantial (Fleiss' κ = 0.71)," plus **per-category agreement** or the main confusion (e.g., stalling vs productive struggle), and how disagreements were resolved (discussion, majority vote) [1]. ### CONV reporting-f1-drop-after-leakage-fix
title: Reporting a metric drop after fixing leakage
start: 2026-07-25 23:10
projects: project_idetrace
---
@user
found a leakage bug in our neurips d&b submission — same students in train and test. fixed with group split by student id, macro f1 0.81 -> 0.74. rebuttal is in 4 days. how do i report this without tanking the paper
@assistant
Report it **proactively and transparently** — reviewers generally respond better to authors who find and fix their own leakage than to ones who get caught [1][2]. A structure that works:

1. **Lead with the correction:** "During the rebuttal period we identified that our random window-level split placed windows from the same student in both train and test. We re-ran all baselines with a split grouped by student id."
2. **Report both numbers** in a small table: original (leaky) 0.81 vs corrected 0.74 macro-F1, for each baseline. Showing all baselines dropping similarly signals the issue was the split, not a single model [2].
3. **Explain why the corrected number matters for a benchmark:** student-grouped evaluation measures generalization to unseen learners, which is the intended use case [1].
4. **Commit to updating the official split** in the released dataset and the datasheet, and add a leakage-check script to the repo.

For a datasets paper, the contribution is the dataset and its evaluation protocol; a lower but honest number on a well-defined split arguably *strengthens* it [2].
[src] Leakage and reproducibility in ML-based science | https://www.mlpitfalls.net/leakage-reproducibility | How leakage inflates results and how to report corrections.
[src] Responding to reviewers when your results change | https://www.academicwritingguide.org/results-changed | Strategies for transparent corrections during review.
@user +5m
should the 0.81 stay anywhere in the camera ready
@assistant
Usually only in a brief note or appendix if at all — e.g., "an earlier version used a window-level split, which overestimated performance (0.81); all reported results use the student-grouped split." Including it once is useful as a **cautionary data point for others using the dataset**, but the headline results should be the corrected ones [1].

Use a **Brewfile** to reinstall cleanly instead of copying `/opt/homebrew` [1][2]:

On the old Mac:
```bash
brew bundle dump --file=~/Brewfile --describe
```
Review the file and delete anything you no longer use. Copy it over (AirDrop, iCloud, git dotfiles repo).

On the new Mac:
```bash
/bin/bash -c "$(curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Homebrew/install/HEAD/install.sh)"
brew bundle --file=~/Brewfile
```
This installs formulae, casks and (optionally) Mac App Store apps listed via `mas` [1]. Things a Brewfile won't move: config files (`~/.zshrc`, `~/.gitconfig`, `~/.ssh` — copy those deliberately), language-version managers' installed versions (pyenv/nvm), and app-specific settings [2].

@user
how to run a 30 min semi structured interview with phd students about how they keep track of research ideas. we're building a tool but i dont want it to be a sales pitch
@assistant
A structure that fits 30 minutes and avoids pitching [1][2]:

**0–3 min — Framing.** Say you're trying to understand how researchers work, there are no right answers, and you'll ask permission before recording. Don't describe your product.

**3–20 min — Recent concrete episodes.** Ask about *specific recent* behavior rather than opinions:
- "Think of the last time you had an idea for a paper. Where did it first get written down?"
- "Walk me through finding something you read months ago. What did you try first?"
- "When did you last lose track of where an idea came from? What happened?"
Follow up with "what happened next?" and "can you show me?" (screen-share their actual notes if they're comfortable).

**20–27 min — Tools and workarounds.** "What tools are involved, and what do you do that feels like a workaround?" Listen for copy-paste chains, duplicate notes, screenshot folders.

**27–30 min — Close.** "Anything I should have asked?" and permission for follow-up.

**Avoid:** leading questions ("Would a tool that X help?"), hypotheticals about future behavior, and showing a demo until after the interview — if at all [2].

A multiverse analysis acknowledges that a single dataset can be analyzed in many defensible ways (different exclusion rules, variable codings, model specifications) [1]. Common guidance for 15-minute research talks [1][2]:
- **~12–16 slides** for content (about one per minute), plus title and a closing slide. Systems talks with demo videos can have fewer slides, since a 60–90 s video replaces several.
- **Structure:** problem (2 min) → key insight (1–2 min) → system walkthrough/demo (4–5 min) → evaluation highlights (4 min) → limitations + takeaway (1–2 min).
